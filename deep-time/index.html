Deep Time — from the Big Bang to this morning

The problem with a timeline of everything is that everything interesting to us happens at the very end. On a normal ruler, all of recorded human history is thinner than a hair. So this chart starts on a logarithmic scale — each step left is ten times older — then lets you drop into real, linear time at any depth to feel how uneven the pacing really is. Every date carries its published uncertainty as a bar and a confidence grade, because on this chart the error on a single event can exceed the entire history of animal life.

How many times has Earth changed its skin?

Two different answers, depending on which skin you mean. The ocean floor — 70% of the surface — is destroyed and rebuilt on a loop. The continents are not: they float too high to be pulled under, so they get folded, stretched and stitched, but never erased. That is why a 4-billion-year-old rock still exists on land and not one square metre of ocean floor older than 200 million years survives anywhere.

≈ 25 coats At today's spreading rates Earth has worn roughly that many complete ocean floors since it formed — and probably more, because the young, hotter planet cycled its plates faster. Pangaea was only the most recent full assembly, the fifth or sixth in the sequence; the next is due in 200–250 million years. Every one of those cycles is longer than the entire age of our species.

Kenorland and Pannotia are the contested ones — their extent, and in Pannotia's case whether it ever fully closed, are still argued. The future supercontinent is a projection with four competing models (Amasia, Pangaea Ultima, Novopangaea, Aurica) that disagree on where it forms, not on whether.

Every event carries a published uncertainty range and a confidence grade. Where sources disagree the midpoint is plotted and the full range drawn as a bar, so a wide bar means genuine scientific disagreement, not sloppiness. Ten events are graded contested — the date, or in some cases whether the event happened at all, is unresolved. Figures for the next supercontinent and the ~25 ocean-floor turnovers are back-of-envelope extrapolations from present-day rates, not measurements.
